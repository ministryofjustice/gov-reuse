# Understanding reuse across government

We’ve been researching how people across government find, share and reuse existing work. This page summarises what
we’ve learned about users, their needs and the barriers to reuse.

## Users and behaviours

We’ve identified 4 emerging behaviour types based on how people approach reuse. These are not fixed groups or job
roles. People may move between them depending on what they’re doing.

![Illustrations of four reuse behaviour types: Deciders need confidence, Makers need existing components and guidance, Learners need to know where to start, and Contributors need to share resources.](/assets/images/research-reuse-behaviour-types.png)

### Deciders

People making decisions about services. They need to manage risks, meet standards and be confident that existing work
is suitable for their service.

### Makers

People designing and developing services. They need to find and use existing components, patterns and guidance to
avoid duplicating work.

### Learners

People building their knowledge of government services and how they work. They need to understand what’s available,
where to start and who can help.

### Contributors

People sharing work that others could reuse. They need clear ways to contribute resources and help others find and
use them.

## User needs and barriers

Our research has identified 6 areas of user need related to reusing existing work, along with common barriers that
can make reuse difficult.

### Finding resources

Resources are spread across departments and websites. People may not know where to search or what terms to use.

People need to:

* find relevant resources before creating something new
* know where to look across government

### Trusting resources

People cannot always tell whether a resource is current, accessible, tested or appropriate for their service.

People need to:

* know whether resources are current and maintained
* understand whether they’ve been researched and tested for accessibility

### Understanding the context

Resources do not always include enough guidance or examples to help people decide whether they can use them.

People need to:

* understand why and how something was created
* see examples of where it has been used

### Connecting with others

People often rely on colleagues and communities to find existing work. This can make reuse harder for those without
established networks.

![Colleagues considering where to find help, including Slack, community calls, word of mouth, team folders and asking someone.](/assets/images/research-reuse-where-to-find-help.png)

People need to:

* find teams working on similar problems
* learn from their experiences and decisions

### Understanding standards and ownership

Differences in how departments organise, describe and maintain resources make it harder to find and reuse work across
government.

People need to:

* know who owns and maintains a resource
* understand how to contribute and what standards to follow

### Using and adapting resources

People need to:

* know when and how to use existing resources
* understand how to adapt them for their service

## What we’ve learned

### People search in different ways

Some people search for a specific component or pattern. Others start with a problem they need to solve. Experience
affects how people search and what guidance they need.

### Finding a resource does not mean someone will use it

People need evidence, examples and information about accessibility and maintenance before deciding whether to reuse
something.

### Communities help people reuse work

People use departmental and cross-government communities to discover resources, ask questions and learn from other
teams.

### Examples help people make decisions

Seeing how a component or pattern has been used in a live service helps people decide whether it could work for them.
Templates support people in creating consistency in government services.

### New starters need clear guidance

People new to government are not always introduced to design systems or other reusable resources. Bringing
information together can help, but too much information can be overwhelming.

## How we’re using our research

Our findings are helping us:

* explore ways to search across government design systems using everyday language
* improve guidance for people getting started with reuse
* understand what information people need before adopting a resource
* make it easier to share and contribute reusable work

## Ongoing research

We’re continuing to research:
